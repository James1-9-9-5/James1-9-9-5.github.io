About Me

My path into GIS wasn't entirely linear. I first came across GIS during my studies at Brighton University, though at the time it never occurred to me that it could actually be a career — it was just a module, not a plan. After graduating, I spent my early twenties bouncing around various roles, trying to unearth a career that suited me, without much luck. It was actually COVID that gave me the pause I needed to properly reconsider what I wanted to do — and somewhere in that reconsideration, GIS resurfaced, this time as a genuine career path rather than just something I'd studied. I took myself to South Africa to complete an internship alongside an Anti-Poaching Unit, and that's really where everything changed.


Living alongside the rangers, waking before dawn to head out into the bush, I found a rhythm and a sense of purpose I hadn't really felt before. It taught me how much I value being useful in a tangible way — not just doing a job, but actually seeing the difference it makes on the ground.


Outside of work, I'm pretty much always trying to be outdoors or moving. I love hiking and bouldering, and you'll usually find me at the gym a few times a week too — I like having something physical to balance out the hours spent staring at a screen mapping things. Travelling is a big one for me as well; I'll take any excuse to go and explore somewhere new, which honestly ties in quite nicely with my love of maps and landscapes in the first place. There's something satisfying about seeing a place with your own eyes after spending so much time looking at it from above.


When I'm not out climbing or halfway up a hill somewhere, I'm happiest spending time with friends and family. That's the stuff that keeps me grounded, especially after a period like my early twenties where I felt a bit lost and unsure of what I was doing with my life. I've come to appreciate that having good people around you matters just as much as figuring out your career does — probably more, if I'm honest.


Looking back, I don't think my path needed to be linear to get me somewhere good. If anything, the detours — the false starts, the pandemic pause, the leap of faith to South Africa — are exactly what shaped who I am today, both in and out of work.


Where I've Worked

My path into GIS wasn't entirely linear. I spent my early twenties in bouncing around various roles, attempting to unearth a career that suits me. Collectivelt, these roles taught me how to manage people, data, and deadlines long before I ever touched a mapping tool professionally. There's a version of this story where none of that experience mattered once I moved into GIS, but the opposite has been true: knowing how to communicate clearly, manage competing priorities, and actually listen to what people need has shaped how I approach every project since.


The real turning point came through conservation work — internships and fieldwork mapping vegetation, tracking endangered species, and supporting anti-poaching efforts. It was there that GIS stopped being just software to me and became a way of understanding a landscape, a population, a problem, all at once. That internship led to technician work classifying land cover, which led to analyst roles where I started solving genuine operational problems with spatial data — automating reports, cleaning up databases, building tools that people actually used day to day.


Today, I lead the design and deployment of full GIS and asset management systems, working across the entire stack from field data collection to dashboards leadership actually looks at. But the instinct that got me here hasn't changed much from those early days in the field: give me a dataset, a map, and a problem worth solving, and I'm happy.

Volunteering

Volunteered for 6 weeks as a Co-ordinator at Parque Machía in Bolivia, a stint nestled into a 4-month solo trip around South America, helping manage the day-to-day running of the sanctuary while supporting the care of rescued wildlife. I worked with an international team to organise daily tasks, oversee volunteers, maintain animal enclosures, and contribute to the rehabilitation and long-term welfare of the animals in a pretty hands-on, challenging environment — and came away with a serious soft spot for one resident in particular, Sheldon the Coati, whose personality made even the toughest days there worth it.
