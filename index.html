The Long Way Round

My journey into GIS wasn’t a linear path; it was a series of perspective shifts. I first encountered mapping as a static module during my Geography degree at Brighton, but back then, it felt like lines on a screen rather than a calling. It took a few years of searching through different roles in my early twenties, followed by the forced pause of the pandemic, to realize what I was actually looking for: a way to connect digital data with real-world impact. That realization led to a leap of faith that took me across the world to South Africa. Joining an Anti-Poaching Unit, I swapped a desk for vast, sun-baked desert dunes, learning to navigate and read a stark, challenging landscape from the ground up.

Living alongside the rangers and waking before dawn to head out into the bush, I found a rhythm and a sense of purpose I hadn't felt before. It taught me how much I value being useful in a tangible way: not just doing a job, but seeing the difference it makes on the ground. That's still what draws me to the work I do today.

Outside of work I'm almost always outdoors or on the move. I love hiking and bouldering, and I'm at the gym a few times a week to balance out the hours spent mapping. I'll take any excuse to travel, which ties in nicely with my love of maps; there's something satisfying about seeing a place with your own eyes after so long looking at it from above. When I'm not halfway up a hill, I'm happiest with friends and family, who keep me grounded. Looking back, the detours (the false starts, the pandemic pause, the leap of faith to South Africa) are exactly what shaped who I am, in and out of work.

Before any of this, I spent three months in early 2019 as a coordinator at Parque Machía in Villa Tunari, Bolivia, working with the park's animals and helping keep things running day to day. It was an early sign of how much I enjoy hands-on work that has a visible impact.
